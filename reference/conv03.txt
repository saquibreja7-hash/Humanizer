In March 2018, Jack Dorsey, the chief executive of Twitter, sat down for a meeting in New Delhi with a group of women writers and activists. Afterwards he posted a photograph of himself holding a handwritten sign. The sign read "Smash Brahminical Patriarchy," and the post set off a storm across Indian social media within hours. Many users saw the image as an endorsement of a slogan that they found offensive, and others saw it as a sign that Twitter was taking sides in a debate about caste that it did not fully understand.

The controversy was messy, and it said a lot about the state of the conversation on the platform. The sign came from a protest by activists who argue that caste oppression is sustained by upper-caste dominance in culture, religion and the economy. For those activists, the phrase was a direct and accurate description of a system they had lived under. For critics, including many Brahmins and some non-Brahmin commentators, the slogan looked like an attack on a whole community based on birth. Both groups were speaking from real experience, and neither was willing to accept the other's framing.

Dorsey later said he had not intended to endorse the slogan, and that he had posted the picture without fully understanding its meaning. The clarification did little to calm the anger. Some users called for a boycott of the platform. Others pointed out that the same people who were outraged by the photo had said little about the everyday discrimination that Dalit users described on the same site. The episode became a kind of mirror, in which each side saw its own assumptions reflected back.

What made the row important was not the sign itself. It was the fact that a global technology company had become a place where caste identity was argued in public, often in harsh terms. For many Indian users, Twitter was the first space where they could speak about caste without the filter of family, school or workplace. For others, it was a place where anonymous accounts could attack people for their surnames, and where quick judgements spread faster than careful reading.

The debate moved quickly from the platform to the workplace. Indian tech companies, multinational firms with Indian offices and startups in Bengaluru and Gurugram employ large numbers of graduates from urban, English-speaking backgrounds. Surveys and anecdotal accounts suggest that caste still matters in hiring, referrals and promotions, even in sectors that present themselves as modern and merit-based. Candidates from backward or Dalit backgrounds often report that their accents, their colleges or their surnames shape how they are treated, and that they feel pressure to hide where they come from.

Companies have been slow to respond. Many have diversity policies that focus on gender, disability and sexual orientation, but few track caste explicitly. Some firms worry that collecting caste data would be intrusive or legally risky. Others argue that caste is not a workplace issue at all, because they hire on merit. Critics reply that merit is itself shaped by caste, since access to good schools, coaching classes and professional networks is unevenly distributed.

Some organisations have started to act. A few companies run training sessions on unconscious bias that include caste as a category. Certain employee groups have pushed for mentorship programmes for Dalit and Adivasi staff. These steps are welcome, but they are small, and they depend on leaders who are willing to talk about caste openly, which is still uncommon.

The Twitter row was briefly a national spectacle. Its longer lesson is less dramatic. Caste does not disappear when people move into offices, apps and cities. It adapts, often quietly, and it shows up in who is invited to speak, who is believed and who is expected to stay silent. Taking that seriously means having conversations that are uncomfortable for everyone involved, and accepting that the same word can mean an injury to one person and an insult to another.
